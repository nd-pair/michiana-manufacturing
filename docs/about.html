About the data

The map shows 951 manufacturers at 1,092 sites in the South Bend–Elkhart region, plus 46 other organizations (research and software firms with no production, investors and universities) that appear on the map but are left out of every company count. Every figure on it comes from a public source, listed below with how it is matched, how current it is, and what it leaves out. Built 2026-11-14.

How the data is collected

Every plant is one file in the repository. Five sources add files; checks on different schedules decide whether each one is shown, flagged as unconfirmed, or kept off the map. Files are never deleted, so a closed plant is not added back by the next import. Counts are from the latest build.

Disclaimer

The visualizations, the code and most of the text on this site were generated with AI tools. The data comes from public sources, almost all of them government datasets (the Department of Homeland Security, OSHA, BLS, the Census Bureau, BEA, USAspending.gov, SBIR.gov, openFDA and Indiana's Department of Workforce Development); a few entries cite company websites or regional news, each named in its file. Records from different sources are matched and combined automatically, by name, address and identifiers, and the scores are computed from them without review of each figure, so the site can contain mistakes: a wrong match, a closed plant still shown, an outdated headcount or a misassigned sector. Corrections are welcome on GitHub.

Headcounts

Column height is headcount on a log scale. Every figure carries a year and a grade, shown in the roster and the detail panel:

  • Estimate. HIFLD's employee figures are modeled estimates; the year shown is the base dataset's (2022), not a survey date. Business-directory ranges are graded the same way.
  • Reported. OSHA Form 300A annual average employees, the establishment's own filing for that year; figures a company states itself (website, filing, press release) or reported on an SBIR award, with that year.
  • Company-wide. Marked separately where only a figure for the whole company exists, as for the Elkhart RV manufacturers.

Staff totals in the panels add the known figures as they are, so they are approximate and mix years.

Capabilities, employment and the timeline

Capabilities are inferred from NAICS codes and product keywords with an editable mapping; a capability with one or no company in the current selection is flagged as a gap. Manufacturing employment is from the BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, private ownership, annual averages, 2014–2025 (pulled 2026-11-12). The sector view sums 3-digit NAICS rows over the region's counties; BLS withholds cells that would reveal a single employer, so sector totals are a lower bound. The workforce panel draws on four BLS programs. Specialization, pay and establishment counts are from the same QCEW files (2025); the location quotient divides an industry's share of the region's private jobs by its share nationally, and region-wide job counts are lower bounds where BLS withholds a county's figure. Occupations are from Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2025) for the South Bend–Mishawaka, Elkhart–Goshen, Niles and Michigan City–La Porte metro areas, which leave out counties such as Kosciusko and Starke; estimates BLS cannot publish are omitted. Monthly manufacturing jobs are from Current Employment Statistics and county unemployment from Local Area Unemployment Statistics, not seasonally adjusted, through September 2026 (pulled 2026-11-13). The timeline lists 802 dated events: federal awards of $250,000 or more, SBIR/STTR awards, milestones toward manufacturing physical AI and, where available, patents.

Humanoid Deployment Benefit Index

The index asks where deploying humanoid robots could benefit the region's manufacturers most: how much local work is the hard, repetitive and hazardous kind a humanoid could take on, how much production is custom and short-run, how tight the labor market is, whether the people who install and maintain automation are here, and how much of a robot could be sourced locally. It is not a count of jobs at risk, and it does not show that workers gain: whether a deployment lets the same or more people make more, or replaces them, is outside what it measures. One of its six pillars tracks manufacturing jobs and pay, but the index is a weighted geometric mean, so gains in the other pillars can outweigh a fall in that one; read that pillar on its own. Five pillars compare the region with the U.S. average, set to 100. Local supply has no U.S. benchmark, so it is scored against a fixed target instead (below), and the index therefore is not a ratio to the U.S. as a whole. A weak pillar pulls the index down more than a strong one lifts it.

  • Task fit: local jobs in production, engineering and material-handling occupations (BLS OEWS, May 2025, the four metro areas) weighted by how much of each occupation's work is handling and moving parts, repetitive motion, bending, kneeling and long standing, and hazardous equipment, fumes and heat, from the O*NET 31.0 work-context and work-activity ratings (CC BY 4.0), per job, against the same measure for the U.S.
  • High-mix potential: the share of manufacturing jobs in custom and short-run industries (machine and job shops, fabrication, molds and dies, RVs and trailers, boats, custom woodwork, medical devices; a lower bound where BLS withholds county figures), and workers per establishment, where smaller scores higher (BLS QCEW).
  • Labor pressure: the unemployment rate (lower scores higher), the share of manufacturing workers aged 55 and older, and quarterly manufacturing turnover, from the Census Bureau's Quarterly Workforce Indicators, averaged over the latest four quarters with data.
  • Adoption readiness: industrial machinery mechanics, electricians, engineering and mechatronics technicians, and industrial, mechanical and electrical engineers per 1,000 jobs. Software developers were left out as of November 13, 2026: they made up 44% of the U.S. count and mostly measure a metro's size and tech sector rather than factory automation skills. With them, the region scored 90 on this pillar and 103 on the index.
  • Local supply: twice the share of a humanoid's cost available from two or more local suppliers, so that half the robot sourced locally scores 100; there is no U.S. benchmark for this pillar.
  • Jobs and pay: growth of manufacturing jobs and average pay over the latest five years against the U.S., and manufacturing value added per job (BEA county GDP over QCEW jobs, for the counties BEA publishes). A fall here lowers the index but can be outweighed by gains in the other pillars.

Weights are equal, and the weights, traits and lists are editable. Each pillar and part is colored in seven bands, from dark green (150 or more) to red (under 60), after the EU energy label: yellow (95 to 104) is about the benchmark (the U.S. level, or for local supply the fixed target), and orange and red mark areas that need work.

History. The index, every pillar and every part are saved each month. For the parts that public data allows, yearly values go back to 2016: the high-mix share and establishment size (QCEW, with the U.S. figures for each year), unemployment (LAUS, the yearly average of the monthly rates; October 2025 was not collected, so 2025 averages eleven months), workers aged 55 and older and turnover (QWI, over quarters that both the region and the U.S. report), five-year job and pay growth, and value added per job. Task fit, adoption readiness and local supply have no public history and start with the monthly record, as does the index, which needs all six pillars.

Region comparison. At the bottom of the Physical AI outlook tab, the same index is computed for ten metro areas of similar size, each with a well-known research university: Lafayette–West Lafayette, Champaign–Urbana, Ann Arbor, Lansing–East Lansing, Madison, Rochester, Dayton, Greenville–Anderson, Knoxville and Cedar Rapids–Iowa City. Each region is a set of counties (for jobs, establishments, pay, unemployment, workforce age and turnover, and value added) and the BLS metro areas that cover them (for occupations); the method, weights and U.S. benchmarks are the same as for the region. It is recomputed with the monthly refresh. Michigan has published no Census workforce quarters since 2023, so Ann Arbor and Lansing score labor pressure on unemployment alone. The list of regions is in data/regions.toml.

Humanoid supply chain

The humanoid panel measures progress toward manufacturing physical AI in the region. It divides a full-size humanoid robot into 20 components (motors, gear reducers, screws, bearings, encoders, drivers, the main computer, control boards, harnesses, battery, cameras, inertial and touch sensors, hand mechanisms, castings, machined parts, sheet metal, plastics, composites and integration), each with a rough share of the robot's cost taken from public teardown estimates. A company counts as having a related capability when its industry code or product description matches the component's rules; this is an inference and overstates readiness, since a company that rebuilds industrial motors is not yet a maker of humanoid joint motors. A part is available now when two or more local companies can supply it today: for build-to-print parts (machined, cast, stamped and molded parts, wiring harnesses, circuit-board assembly) any capable contract shop qualifies, since it works from the robot maker's drawings; for motors, reducers, sensors, the main computer and the other engineered parts, only a company whose record confirms it makes the part counts. Both shares are saved every month to show the trend. Robotics-related awards are the federal awards of $250,000 or more and the SBIR/STTR awards on the timeline whose titles mention robots, actuators, motors or sensors.

Known gaps

  • HIFLD (base year 2022) omits some small shops and lags openings and closures, sometimes by years. OSHA's yearly filings add plants with 20 or more employees, but no free source names smaller shops. Closures are flagged for review, never applied automatically: from Indiana's WARN layoff and closing notices (54 manufacturing notices since 2016, on the timeline), from plants that stop filing with OSHA, and from websites that no longer resolve. Michigan publishes its WARN notices only through a search page, so the three Michigan counties lack that signal. A facility confirmed closed is marked in its file and left off the map (62 so far). Because listing a plant that does not exist is worse than missing one, an imported record that a verification check finds closed without a dated source, or with no current trace at its address, is presumed closed and also left off (374 so far); a later OSHA filing or a dated source brings it back. Where the company plainly operates in the region but no current source confirms a plant at the listed address, the entry stays on the map marked unconfirmed, as a dim column with a note on its card (59 entries); a November 2026 accuracy audit found about 40% of such entries wrong where they could be checked. The audits are in the repository's audits folder.
  • Entries that should not be on the map at all (duplicates, sites outside the region, offices that make nothing) are marked removed with a reason and left off (87 so far).
  • Missing or doubtful facts are kept on a public list of open items (83 now): a company without a street address, an address the geocoders cannot place, a website whose domain no longer exists or is parked, and a status note marked unconfirmed or disputed. Each monthly review tries once to fix each one; after three attempts the doubtful fact is removed. A dead website link or an unconfirmed note is deleted, an address without a street number is dropped so the company stays at its city's center, and a full address that cannot be placed is kept as it is.
  • Sectors for SBIR firms are assigned from the words in their award titles and may be wrong for firms whose awards span fields.
  • Name matching can still miss a federal recipient that uses a different legal name and has no UEI on file.
  • Certifications other than FDA registration (AS9100, ISO 9001, Nadcap, CMMC) are not in open registries and appear only where a company publishes them.

Automated news review

Each month an automated review reads the region's news with Anthropic's Claude. Its web search and page reading are limited to 19 trusted sources: regional newsrooms (WSBT, WNDU, ABC57, the Elkhart Truth, the Goshen News, InkFreeNews, News Now Warsaw, WKVI, the Michigan City News-Dispatch, the Herald-Palladium and Leader Publications), business and trade publications (Inside Indiana Business, the Indianapolis Business Journal and the RV trade press), and state and university news sites. The South Bend Tribune and MLive block automated reading and cannot be used.

The review searches the past month's news for closures, new plants and companies, acquisitions, renames and moves, and robotics and physical-AI milestones. It also rechecks companies whose websites have gone dark and works through the open items. A finding changes the map only if checks in code confirm it: the article is on a trusted site and was actually retrieved, the fact is stated outright with a date, and it matches exactly one company. It follows the written rules in the repository's CURATION.md. Each change carries its source and is logged with the run's cost; files are never deleted, and a run stops before it could spend more than $20.

Refresh and reuse

A scheduled job refreshes every source on the 2nd of each month (HIFLD, SBIR.gov, USAspending, openFDA, the BLS programs, O*NET, the Census workforce indicators and BEA), scans every company website, runs the news review and rebuilds the site. The data files and scripts are public at github.com/nd-pair/michiana-manufacturing; the map's roster downloads as CSV with every field, and any filtered view can be shared as a link.
